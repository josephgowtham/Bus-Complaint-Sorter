# Project Reflection: Bus Complaint Sorter

**Programme:** AI-Native Engineering Programme – Semester 1  
**Institution:** Coimbatore Institute of Technology (CIT)  
**Author:** AI Application Engineering Student  

Building the Bus Complaint Sorter has provided profound insights into the real-world operational challenges of integrating Large Language Models into mission-critical campus infrastructure. Prior to this capstone project, I perceived AI engineering primarily through the lens of prompt design. However, developing this system demonstrated that the prompt represents merely ten percent of an enterprise AI pipeline; the true engineering challenges lie in validation boundaries, fail-safe architectures, and institutional governance.

Our primary objective was to triage student feedback across CIT’s twelve bus routes, distinguishing routine operational complaints—such as delays and lost items—from critical safety emergencies such as rash driving, brake failures, and hazardous footboard travel. One of the most critical lessons learned was the necessity of a "zero-trust" approach to LLM outputs. In our initial test runs, we observed that while the Gemini API exhibited high semantic fluency with colloquial Tamil-English (Tanglish) phrasing, it occasionally hallucinated route numbers by inferring them from geographic landmarks rather than explicit student testimony. In an actual transport depot, dispatching maintenance crews or mechanics based on an artificial hallucination creates operational chaos and misallocates resources. 

To overcome this, we engineered a deterministic Python validation gatekeeper (`is_valid()`). This function enforces a dual-verification rule: a route is only approved if it exists in the validated `routes.csv` database and its numeric representation is verified directly within the raw text string of the student's submission. Furthermore, we designed a resilient fallback mechanism. If the network drops or API quotas are exhausted during a campus emergency, the application seamlessly shifts to deterministic keyword triage, ensuring that urgent safety reports are never dropped and the application never terminates prematurely.

Equally transformative was understanding ethical boundaries in automated systems. As engineers, there is often a temptation to automate end-to-end workflows, including notifying drivers directly or assigning fault. Adhering to the CIT institutional governance guidelines—mandating that all final decisions reside strictly with the Human Transport Officer—reinforced that AI must serve as an amplifier of human capability rather than an unaccountable decision-maker. Ultimately, this project demonstrated that resilient AI systems are defined not by model scale, but by the rigor of their architectural guardrails, graceful error handling, and unwavering commitment to safety.
